The Comeback CEO: What HR Can Learn From Nvidia's Reversal

Jensen Huang admitted he was unfaithful, resigned, and was reinstated less than two weeks later

Share on X · Share on LinkedIn · Share on Facebook

Less than two weeks after Jensen Huang resigned, Nvidia's board brought him back. The chipmaker at the center of the AI boom announced on November 23, 2026, that its board had voted unanimously to reinstate Huang as president, CEO and board member, effective immediately.

Every HR professional reading the news is asking the same question: would a regular employee get the same outcome?

What we know

Huang resigned following reports that he had an affair with a woman suspected of being a Chinese spy, NPR reports. In a memo to employees, he wrote, "I was unfaithful to my wife," apologized, and said he supported an independent investigation, according to NBC News. As recently as November 17, the board said it did not intend to reinstate him.

In its announcement, Nvidia said the board was satisfied with Huang's candor and ability to lead after a series of meetings with him, and that it received input from "senior levels of the United States government." President Trump publicly called the reinstatement the "correct and proper decision." The board's investigation, led by a committee of independent directors with outside counsel, will continue to its conclusion. Nvidia also said it is creating a new Chief Compliance Officer position.

Five HR lessons

  • Consistency is the policy. If a rule bends for top performers, employees notice, and inconsistent treatment is a common thread in discrimination and retaliation claims. Decide in advance who handles misconduct at the executive level and how.

  • Finish the investigation before the final decision. Reinstating someone while an investigation is open is a judgment call. If your organization ever makes it, document why, and what happens if the findings change the picture.

  • Use independent investigators at the top. Nvidia used independent directors and outside counsel. A small company can't form a board committee, but it can bring in an outside investigator when the person under review is the boss.

  • Write down outside pressure. Investors, customers, celebrities and even officials may weigh in. Base the decision on the facts, and record what input you received and how you weighed it.

  • Give compliance an owner. Nvidia is adding a Chief Compliance Officer. In a small organization that may be the HR department of one, but someone needs clear responsibility and a direct line to leadership.

The bottom line

Boards can reinstate leaders, and Nvidia's board has made its call. For the rest of us, the lesson is simpler: the rules people see you apply at the top are the rules they believe you have.

Would your organization handle this the same way for every employee?
